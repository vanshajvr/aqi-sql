Three things to know

  1. Winter smog is mostly the weather. Shallow winter air traps pollution. December is only 9–15% above what its weather predicts.
  2. Crop burning adds a spike the weather can't explain. Late October to mid-November runs up to 2.2× the weather-predicted level.
  3. PM2.5 is regional; traffic pollution is local. The 2020 lockdown cut NO2 by 41–57% but PM2.5 by only 20–47%.

$event
How this is measured One city-wide AQI per day (mean of reporting stations, days with ≥5 stations). CPCB categories: Very Poor 301–400, Severe 401+. 2020 lockdown excluded. 95% intervals: December 68–93%, March–September 9–19%.

Shallow air, dirtier air

The lower the mixing height, the higher the PM2.5. October and November sit above the pattern.

$weather_monthly
How this is measured Mixing height is the depth of air that pollution can spread into (ERA5 reanalysis, central Delhi). April–June are dustier than their mixing height suggests because of pre-monsoon dust storms.

Pollution beyond the weather

Late October to mid-November reaches 2.2× what the weather predicts: the crop-burning window.

$weather_excess
How this is measured Dry days only. Each day is compared with typical days that had the same mixing height and wind, using every month except October–November as the baseline. 1× means the weather fully explains the pollution. Diwali falls in the same window. 95% intervals: late October 1.3–2.1×, early November 1.7–3.0×.

Crop-fire smoke rides the wind

After heavy burning in Punjab, Delhi's PM2.5 reaches 2.1× the weather prediction when the wind blows from Punjab. In other winds the effect is small.

$fires
How this is measured Daily satellite fire counts in Punjab and northern Haryana (NASA FIRMS, VIIRS), the day before. Each bar compares PM2.5 with same-weather days, as in "Pollution beyond the weather". 15 October – 30 November, dry days only, and the weeks around Diwali removed: crackers are local smoke that needs no north-westerly, and leaving them in hides the wind pattern. With the wind from Punjab, the rise from fewest to most fires is +0.69× (95% interval +0.06 to +1.26). The difference from other winds is suggestive but not proven (90% of resamples).

The 2020 lockdown test

Traffic (NO2) and dust (PM10) fell most. About half of the PM2.5 stayed.

$lockdown
How this is measured 25 March – 3 May 2020 against the same dates in 2019, same stations. Grey: how much lower 2020 already was on 1–21 March. Blue: the extra drop during the lockdown, a conservative lower bound. White diamonds: a second estimate comparing dry days with same-weather days. Resampling stations, NO2 fell further than PM2.5 in all 500 resamples.

Local or regional?

PM2.5 is similar everywhere (1.8× spread). NO2 varies 4.8× between stations.

$fingerprint
How this is measured Each dot is one station's 2018–19 mean divided by the median of all stations. A narrow spread means a regional pollutant; a wide spread means local hotspots. SO2 is low everywhere.

The Diwali spike

The week after Diwali averages 1.55× the AQI of the weeks before.

$diwali
How this is measured Anchored on each year's actual Diwali date: 3 weeks before = days −21 to −8, week before = −7 to −1, Diwali week = day 0 to +7. 2016 has no data, and 2017's baseline rests on 2 days. Diwali overlaps stubble season, so not all of the jump is firecrackers.

Is Delhi's winter air better than before 2020?

Not yet measurably. The worst days are rarer, but for the same weather, winter pollution is about where it was. Grey bars: 2018–20. Blue: after. Dotted line: the 2018–20 average.

$then_now_severe
$then_now_ratio
How this is measured The same 12 stations in every winter (November–February), so a changing station mix can't fake a trend. Post-2020 data comes from OpenAQ and was checked against the official data on January–June 2020 before use (PM2.5 r = 0.97, median difference 3.9%). Severe days fell 11 points (95% interval −19 to −3); the weather-adjusted change is −0.09 (−0.32 to +0.16), not distinguishable from zero. Three winters (2022–23 to 2024–25) have no data: OpenAQ lost the feed, and a stand-in monitor failed its pre-registered test.

Fires fell 90%. The smoke window didn't.

Satellite-detected crop fires collapsed after 2021, but Delhi's pollution in the smoke window, relative to its weather, was the same in 2025 as in 2018–21.

$then_now_stubble
How this is measured Top: fires detected by NASA's VIIRS satellite in Punjab and northern Haryana, 15 October – 30 November. Bottom: Delhi PM2.5 from 16 October to 15 November, divided by what the day's weather predicts, on the same 12-station panel. 2025 minus 2018–21: −0.006 (95% interval −0.36 to +0.29). No PM2.5 points for 2015–16 (too few panel stations yet) or 2022–24 (no data in the window). Satellites only see fires burning at overpass time, and burning has reportedly shifted later in the day, so falling counts may partly be fires going unseen.

Which rule should send a "bad air tomorrow" alert?

The obvious rule (B: today was bad) never warns before the first bad day. Rule E warned before 69% of them, within the false-alarm budget.

$alert_tradeoff

A every day, November–January

B today was Very Poor (AQI > 300)

C today was Poor or worse (AQI > 200)

D forecast: low mixing height, no rain

E C and D together chosen

F B or D

G B, or C and D together

How this is measured Each rule decides in the evening whether to alert for tomorrow; a hit is a city-wide AQI above 300. "First bad days" are the start of each bad spell (today fine, tomorrow bad): the warning that changes what people do. Rules and the choice criterion were fixed in advance: on 2015–17, pick the best first-bad-day rate with at most 4 false alerts a month. Scores shown are 2018–20, which played no part in the choice. The forecast is the actual next-day weather, so real-world scores would be somewhat lower. Rule E: 34 of 49 first bad days (95% interval 55–80%); rule B: 0 of 49 (at most 7%).
